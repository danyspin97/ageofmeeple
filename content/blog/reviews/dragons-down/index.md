## Being hunted by monsters in a land of pure luck
 
How to describe *Dragons Down*? It's big, massive, and it attempts to reach a new
level of sandboxing, mimicking its predecessor, {{ bgg(name="Magic Realm",
id="22") }}. At its core it's a fantasy roleplaying game, where you start by
choosing your lineage and class, and depart to find fame and glory, finding
items, and hunting monsters. No, literally — you depart to find Fame that will
lead to **Legend Points**, the victory points of the game. It's not meant to be
balanced, but it has to be taken as an experience; you will adventure through
these wild lands, and, as any good adventure, you don't know where you're
going. These lands are **dangerous**, to the point where the death of your
character(s) is not only contemplated, but even featured in the rulebook.
 
Will you be able to find glory? More importantly, will you manage to survive
these harsh lands? Be sure to bring a lucky charm, because things are rough in
*Dragons Down*.
 
## Gameplay
 
{% alert(note=true) %}
The rules will not be explained in full in order to keep this review concise.
Be sure to read the [rulebook] or watch the [How to play video] (the only one at
the time of writing) to learn about all edge cases and rules not covered here.
 
[rulebook]: https://www.scribd.com/document/681710978/Dragons-Down-Rules-v1-0

Let's start with the gameplay and mechanics first, as they only serve to
create the experience, which we are going to discuss extensively later.
 
![](https://cf.geekdo-images.com/ZWBeQptsMtsbhdKDzHkM0g__imagepage/img/8JyuDO4FwCR-Sqe73H-GwZCi8Ng=/fit-in/900x600/filters:no_upscale():strip_icc()/pic7470609.png#end)
 
Each player controls one character, and every character has a **lineage** and a
**class**. A lineage could be a *Human*, a *Dwarf*, an *Elf* or the likes, as
*Dragons Down* doesn't shift from the classic Fantasy setting. The lineage
gives your character one unique ability, which will be very useful during the
game, as well as some partial stats. On the other hand, the class gives your
character the starting items and most of its stats. *Dragons Down* has the
classic Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma — too bad they're used in just 2
different occasions throughout the game: to make haggling easier when we
want to buy from a merchant, or to get more points when solving quests. Given
that we have no idea what quests we will see, they are not very useful overall.
However — and here comes the best part — instead of Strength and Dexterity,
there are different cubes given to your character: *mighty cubes*, used for
dealing damage during combat; *speed cubes*, which are used to determine how
fast you want to attack/evade; *spell cubes*, which come in all sorts of
colors. In addition, there are life points and two very important tracks:
attack speed and maneuver speed. These two starting cards are **the most
important choice of the game**. Build a character that is not well prepared,
and you will die easily. This also means that some combinations are easier
than others, and that you should only experiment after a couple of games or
more.
 
### It's time to take action
 
In *Dragons Down* there are just a bunch of available actions you can do; they
are all thematic, and you can do up to 4 per turn. For example, you can **Move**
through the various zones, though mountains will be harder to traverse and
therefore more expensive in terms of movement points, costing 2 of your
precious 4 movement points you get every action; passing through caves or even
starting your movement in a cave will slow you down, making you lose one
movement point per action. You can **Search** to activate a hidden passage in
your zone, enabling fast movement between two distant zones, which is crucial
if you don't want to spend turns just going around. Search is used to find a
Site in your zone, if your tile / zone contains one, enabling you to **Loot**,
which is another action, and grab whatever treasure might be there. As you can
see, all actions are thematic and have some specific rulings to give the
feeling of living in a fantasy world.
 
Almost everything is done by rolling dice, and the default rule is that we
throw two dice and get the worst result. In other games this is called
disadvantage, and here in *Dragons Down* we spend our entire game playing at
disadvantage. Do not lose hope, though — even monsters have disadvantage when
they throw, so they will still pick the worst result for them. This world is
truly unforgiving. There are a few ways to mitigate the dice, depending on
which action you are doing. If you do a Search action, you need to get a
"found" face on the dice; we are at a disadvantage so we throw two, and if we
get even just one blank, we failed. However, you can keep one symbol if you do
another Search action on the same turn. So probably in 2-3 actions you will
have found whatever you are searching for, but you need to start your turn
with a Search action, and probably use it all just for that.
 
Speaking of the map, it's a hexagon-based one, with each tile containing
multiple zones inside, where your character will actually move. There are
different terrain sets (*Plains*, *Mountains*, and *Caves*) in the base game,
and each comes with its own hexagons: 5 *wild* tiles with 6 different zones
inside, and 1 *civilized*, with just 4 and a fortress for the *natives*, the
inhabitants of the land. The map is built anew every game, either by exploring
it or by creating a layout during setup, so there is a lot of replayability in
that. Either way, you need to set some personal goal or objective for your
character, because movement is restricted and you'll probably struggle a lot
with it, as the map is big. I actually liked the map, though it's a little bit
generic, outside of assigning types to the different zones (mountain, cave,
etc.). What is actually random on the map? Just a single token you draw
randomly the first time any player explores a tile; this token can represent a
site — meaning you need to draw a site for the current terrain type and put it
in its corresponding zone — a gate connecting to another gate in some part of
the map, or it can simply be empty. That's the only random thing on the tile,
which is quite weak.
 
The native fortresses are just one in each set, and if you want to buy/sell
something or to get a quest, you will need to visit them. Probably multiple
times every game. Every fortress comes with its own inhabitants, which is nice
at least. Outside of that, and the different merchants you will find, there
aren't many differences. The quests will be drawn from the same deck, same for
the items at the merchants. The only difference between merchants will be the
statistic that helps you haggle the price of the items. By default all items
available in a merchant can be bought by paying 3x the printed price, during a
**Trade** action. However, you can reduce the price by Haggling: you throw the
dice (at disadvantage here too) and you need to get the same value as your
attribute, or lower (e.g. a value of 2 if you are checking Charisma and your
character has 2 symbols). If you lose, you can try again with another action
on the same turn — except if you get a closed-door symbol, where the merchant
is irritated with you and doesn't want to make any deal until the next turn.
Yes, if it wasn't clear by now, *Dragons Down* is a push-your-luck game, and a
very punishing one at that.
 
### Loot, loot, loot
 
One of the most interesting parts of the game is the items, of which there are
a ton. The way to get them is through the merchant or by doing the Loot action
in a site (disadvantage here too!). These items vary a lot — they can be all
kinds of swords, axes, or even magic items that can give you spell cubes. It's
always exciting drawing new items, hoping to get something good, and then
adapting your build to the new loot. On the other hand, if you find an *Epic*
item, **you will get one Legend Point and five Fame for free**, just because
you were lucky. I think this is one of the points that **detracts the most
from the experience**. Why did I earn that? Just because I was lucky enough to
pull a good item? Sure, in the sites there are Deep Treasures that have more
Epic items, but then the game becomes a hunt for Epic items. That's how my
game went: I was just looting good stuff, I earned a lot of points, and I was
able to arrive second by one point — even though my character died halfway
through.
 
Adapting your build is also said to be one of the most fun parts, at least
according to players who have played a lot. What do you do with a magic staff
when you are a Knight? Oh wait, that tome gives you a purple spell cube; that
scroll lets you learn a magic spell; maybe I can learn this one that only
costs two purple cubes. I'd say this is where *Dragons Down* actually shines
the most. You need to do your best with what you've been given, considering
also your starting lineage and class (which give basically all your character
statistics).
 
### Hey, don't eat my breastplate!
 
These wild monsters are wandering around, looking for players to kill — so how
do we fight them? The combat in *Dragons Down* is one I have never seen
before, and it's very interesting on paper. The mighty cubes are used on
weapons to choose how hard you are hitting; the speed cubes decide how fast
you are hitting and how fast you are evading the attack. However, only
**5 cubes at most can be used in a
combat round**. All monsters also have the same 3 statistics printed on them, so
you know how fast they are attacking and how fast they are evading. However,
you throw the dice at the start of each combat round to determine if an enemy
will change its pattern — in other words, change its face. You can look at
both before deciding how to assign your cubes, but you need to consider what
happens if the monster changes its pattern or not.
 
Now comes the difficult part: how to resolve combat. These statistics are
intertwined — first you need to look at the speed of both attacks; whoever has
the highest goes first. If there is a tie, then check the length of the
monster's attack and of your weapon. Then, whoever goes first needs to check
their speed against the maneuver of the enemy, and if it is higher, the attack
is a success. If it is not, dice will be thrown, with a better chance of
success if the speeds were the same. Perfect, your attack was a success. Now
you need to take into account the armor of the monster: is the damage enough
to kill it? If that's not the case, you have only inflicted a single wound, no
matter how much damage you did. The monster then attacks — same thing as
before, but reversed. If it hits you, you will either die or take a wound.
Taking a wound means moving a cube (which also includes your hearts) to the
wounded space. The more wounds you take, the less functional you are during
combat, meaning you will take more wounds, and so on. You can use your armor
to defend and avoid dying, but the armor might be destroyed if the attack was
stronger than its defense value. If the attack and the defense are the same,
then the armor gets damaged, and you still take a wound. This continues until
either the monsters are dead or you die — or, if no one gets hit in two
rounds, it also ends. At least you get some Fame from the monster when you
win!
 
When do the monsters spawn? When all players have played their turn, then
there is the monster activation phase and combat. You throw the summoning dice
and see if any symbol matches a tile where a player is standing. If they
match, then you get a monster summoned on your zone. Right after that, it will
attack you. You can try to hide (which is not evading), but you need to throw
dice for it. This is where the most important action of the game comes from:
*Sneak*. By sneaking, you can ignore one symbol on the two dice thrown to try
hiding. Most of the time you will get away with it if you do a *Sneak*, but
that also means your 4 actions are instead 3 actions plus a *Sneak*. This is
very random — you can build a story of encountering monsters like that, but
it's still very bland in my opinion.
 
If you are hidden during a combat, and have a ranged weapon, you can do an
ambush and attack the monster. You still need to throw dice every combat round
to see if you remain hidden. What if you want to run? Then you need to have a
higher maneuver speed than the highest maneuver speed of the enemies; that can
be quite difficult if your character has only limited space available in the
maneuver track (I am looking at you, Knight!). That means if you encounter
three enemies at once, your chances are very slim. If you can't even escape,
then consider your character dead. At the same time, there are some
characters — like the Rogue — that have such a high maneuver speed that they
can escape most monsters, avoiding virtually any fight. It means that
character will go on evading monsters the whole game, just grabbing loot, and
maybe making more Legend Points than the others.
 
## The adventure
 
The lack of any type of event is astonishing. I have been playing a campaign
of *Arydia*, and every time you travel you draw one card; depending on the
symbol on the road and the card, some things might happen. I would have loved
to have something similar for *Dragons Down* — something to make the game more
dynamic outside of visiting new tiles and spawning monsters. Something that
makes the game feel like an adventure, where random events might happen: you
might encounter some NPCs with weird offers, a wandering merchant, or you
might even be given a quest or a choice to fight some monsters to help a
person in danger. Yet, all that the adventure of *Dragons Down* amounted to,
for me at least, is a long slog of traveling through sites, hoping to draw
some treasure that gives you points, and avoiding fights if possible.
 
One way to end playing with a character is to retire, but only when you are in
a civilized location. By retiring, you count all the Legend Points you made,
transform Fame into Legend Points at a 10:1 ratio, and — if it is the last
turn — get one more Legend Point.
 
The death mechanism, on the other hand, puts a hard reset on your game. You
only get to keep half of your hard-earned points. Then you take a new
character and depart on a new adventure; if it happens after one third of the
game, it will be very hard to get back on track, basically making you lose the
game. *Dragons Down* has to be played for the experience, and the death of a
character definitely gives you a story to tell. But at the same time, it might
feel like the conclusion of your game and time to pack up — or time to wait
for the others to finish the game. This is punishing just for the sake of it.
A quick solution would be to have an inheritance system, where you either get
all the items you collected or half of the victory points your deceased
character had. That way, dying would still sting, but at least it wouldn't
reset your game.
 
One thing that might seem interesting at first is the *Quests*, which can
only be taken at the fortresses, from the merchant; you can have a maximum of
two quests active at once. The quests are very bland — they contain just brief
instructions, no lore or story to hook you in, and the reward itself is just
Legend Points depending on your and the quest's attribute. From a game that is
all about the experience, I expected a little bit more, unfortunately. Some
quests also reward you with an Outlaw token, meaning that you have done
something bad. Is it good? Well, when your adventure finishes and you retire,
you will throw the dice — it can either give you a Legend Point, take one
away, or just... nothing.
 
## Art and production
 
The art has been made with AI, and that is easy to spot. I am not particularly
against AI — though I prefer human-made art, AI can help in cases like
*Dragons Down*, where commissioning art for the entire game would cost so much
that we might not have a game at all. However, the art is too generic and
lacks life; it lacks an identity. All the characters are generic fantasy
tropes, and the monsters, too, can be found in any fantasy literature — there
isn't even one case where the game tries to add something more. The cards,
which should be the core of the game, don't give you the same excitement when
you pull them, because they aren't as cool-looking as you'd expect. The
iconography works but it's generic, and could have been more descriptive.
 
On the other hand, the monster fiches are awesome — they take the exact amount
of space they should, taking into account all the information there is on
them. It's quite fun to move them around when they spawn or when they are
following you, as well as seeing them in front of your character fighting.
The rest of the components are functional and work well.
 
## Conclusion
 
I had a rough time tracking down a copy of *Dragons Down*. The game has only
ever been available through Kickstarter and through the few merchants who
grabbed a copy. Even buying it used, the price was the same as a new copy — if
you could manage to find somebody selling it. It's a rare game, and it will
probably always be, considering its nature. Its rarity only added more hype
and FOMO for me. The designer put a lot of effort into it, and you can feel
his passion. You can even reach out to him on BGG, where he will gladly answer
any question you have about the game. He's also published a roadmap for new
content, and the plan is to release tons of it. Maybe the expansions will fix
some of my issues.
 
*Dragons Down* takes the legacy of its predecessor, *Magic Realm*, and tries
to bring the same, or even an improved, experience to the 21st century. Its
world is vast, and, as a true sandbox, offers a multitude of ways to customize
your experience — going from an adventuring game to an exploration game,
throwing spells and weapons against monsters. The game mechanics are deep and
complex; there are still tons of rules I didn't mention in this review, like
how ranged damage works (you throw more dice that go from -2 to +2), Undead,
how Spells work (you use your cubes to activate them at the start of the
combat round), Flying, or how to enhance a tile. There are also tons of
optional rules, like *PvP* and *Luring* monsters. However, it is too
complicated and too faithful to how an actual fantasy world would work, for
its own good. Playing the first game and teaching it is work, and you need to
be well prepared and power through. I had fun, yes. However, after thinking
about it later, I realized I had spent more time learning and teaching the
game than actually playing it. It might be a good solo option, or a lifestyle
game for people willing to commit to it, but I will never bring this out to
play as a one-shot with new players, if they aren't interested in playing
again.
 
Another big issue of *Dragons Down* is that the game mechanisms are
interconnected with rules that refer to other rules, which is usually not
only okay, but a good sign of good design. However, all the mechanisms here
are full of edge cases that you need to remember and consider when playing,
and since everything is so interconnected and convoluted, it quickly becomes
hard to play. Add the messy rulebook, and it quickly becomes a disaster. In my
playthrough, I had to go back and forth through the rulebook for at least half
of our three-and-a-half-hour play session. If I'm reading about what token to
place, I don't want to go back to the start to find a small paragraph where
this is properly explained — I'd at least like a reference to the page.
 
The game is big and had all the premises to bring nights of fun ahead, thanks
to its sandbox structure and unique gameplay. However, what I found was a
messy rulebook, a stretched-out night, and a punishing game. What you need the
most is to be lucky: lucky to find rare treasures that will boost your fame,
lucky to not encounter 3 monsters at the same time, and lucky to understand
the rulebook after a single read.
